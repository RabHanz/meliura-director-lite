# Creative brief: independent bouldering gym, stills and carousels

*Researched 23 September 2026. I opened 31 pages; the list is at the bottom. You sent no screenshots, so section 5 is short and I couldn't work out your register. That is explained below.*

---

## 1. Make this first

**Make this first: "The label."** Each problem from the latest reset gets a museum object label.

**Why this one first:** Setting as authorship is being argued in the trade press right now. There was a Climbing Business Journal podcast on 10 September framing setting as writing, and a Gripped piece on 7 July comparing setters to composers. On 4 September, CBJ named routesetting as one of the things gyms should protect in a downturn, and said to look after the members they already have first. Your members climb a couple of times a week, which makes them the audience for this. The format needs only what the gym already has: one wall photo and one sentence from a setter. Evidence and feasibility pointed the same way, so there was no trade-off to make.

**Format:** a four-slide carousel.

**Cover:** one problem, photographed straight on and flat, with the wall filling the frame. In one corner, small, sits a label built the way the V&A builds a "tombstone": what it is, where, when, who made it. For the gym that means the hold colour and wall, *set by [setter's name]*, the date it went up, and the date it comes down. There is no headline. The label is the only type on the cover.

**Slides:**
- **2 (intention):** one sentence from the setter about what they wanted the move to feel like, in their words and credited to them. Nothing else on the slide.
- **3 (the crux):** a crop of the one move the problem is built around. La Fabrique Verticale calls this "the business". Leave the move unexplained. The slide shows where it is.
- **4 (the rest of the wall):** the other problems on that wall this week, as a column of the same small labels. This slide tells members there is more up there than the one you featured.

**Hook:** no hook line. The shape comes from a museum wall: plain facts, set small, on an image you're trusted to look at. The comes-down date should be real. Get it from the gym's reset schedule, not from this brief.

**Caption job:** ask members who has sent it, and get the setter to answer in the comments. CBJ's <creator> episode argues gym social media works "one-to-one" more than "one-to-many".

**What would make it fail:** putting the grade in big type, or adding adjectives ("spicy!", "juggy fun"). Once the label starts selling, it stops being a label. The format also fails without the setter's own sentence. Don't write that sentence for them.

## 2. It works in another niche. Here is your version.

### A. The museum tombstone label
**Source:** V&A, "Writing Labels & Gallery Text", <creator>, 31 Oct 2013 → https://www.vam.ac.uk/blog/creating-new-europe-1600-1800-galleries/labels-gallery-text (opened 23 Sep 2026)

**The pattern:** a small block of plain facts (what it is; where, when and by whom it was made; materials and technique) placed beside an object that gets the whole frame. The facts hold no opinion. The object does the persuading, and the label gives it an author.

**Your version:** see section 1. The object is the problem, and "materials and technique" becomes the wall angle and hold type.

**Why it transfers:** a boulder problem is a made object with a maker and a date, and hardly anyone outside the setting team knows that. The label says it without making an argument.

### B. The chess daily puzzle: "White to move"
**Source:** Chess.com, "How to Solve the Chess.com Daily Puzzle", NM <creator>, updated 13 Mar 2025 → https://www.chess.com/article/view/how-to-solve-the-chess-com-daily-puzzle (opened 23 Sep 2026)

**The pattern:** one position shown from the solver's side ("the solving side will always be heading up the board"), a two-word instruction, and the answer held back. Difficulty rises through the week, from "a Monday puzzle, intended to be the most beginner-friendly" to "a master-level Sunday sizzler".

**Your version:** a photo taken from where a climber stands at the start, looking up at the start holds. Across the bottom, one short instruction with no question mark. The solution (the beta) goes on slide 2. Run it as a weekly ladder: the first post uses the easiest circuit, and each later post in the week goes up a colour.

**Slides:** cover: the start, from the climber's eye line. 2: the first two moves, marked. 3: the crux. 4: where on the floor to find it.

**Hook:** a bare imperative in the source's shape ("[Colour] to move."), not a question.

**Why it transfers:** climbers already call them *problems*. A still image of a start position with the answer held back is exactly how a chess puzzle works, and it needs no motion.

### C. The bookshop staff pick
**Source:** Publishers Weekly *ShelfTalker*, "The Power of the Staff Picks Shelf", <creator>, 1 Mar 2010 → http://blogs.publishersweekly.com/blogs/shelftalker/?p=671 (opened 23 Sep 2026). A record-shop version: "A staff pick with the actual story of why it matters to them" → https://www.socialmon.ai/blog/70-record-store-instagram-post-ideas-with-examples-that-sell-vinyl (opened 23 Sep 2026, undated).

**The pattern:** a small card with the staff member's name on it, one or two sentences on what the thing is and one or two on why *they* love it, placed under the object. PW advises rotating picks every two weeks.

**Your version:** "Front desk pick". A named member of staff, not a setter, picks one problem on the easiest two circuits. Their card says what it is and why they like it. The card sits over a photo of that problem.

**Why it transfers:** the card's value comes from a named person you could meet at the counter. The gym has one, and for someone who has never climbed, a named person is less intimidating than a grade.

### D. The Swiss railway departure poster
**Source:** SBB Visual Information System, Josef Müller-Brockmann and Peter Spalinger, 1978, built on "a simple grid and the Helvetica typeface" → https://peoplesgdarchive.org/item/17132/swiss-federal-railways-sbb-visual-information-system (opened 23 Sep 2026). SBB's own page confirms the departure posters are "the yellow SBB timetables" → https://www.sbb.ch/en/travel-information/rail-traffic-information/timetable-data-pdf/departure-posters.html (opened 23 Sep 2026).

**The pattern:** a timetable as a poster: one typeface, one grid, and one ground colour that means "departures". Every row has the same structure, and the information is the whole design.

**Your version:** "Departures". The reset schedule as one flat-ground card. Each row is a wall, its circuit colours, and the day it comes down, in one typeface on a strict grid. Use one ground colour for it every week, so the colour itself starts to mean "leaving soon".

**Why it transfers:** a reset schedule already is a timetable. It also speaks to a real frustration: the Butora piece (8 Nov 2023) is written for climbers who are "bummed out" when a reset takes their project. This post warns them ahead of time.

### Refused

- **SBB split-flap departure boards.** `medium does not carry`: their appeal is the flaps turning, and a carousel can't reproduce that. The static yellow poster (D) is the part that carries.
- **Personalised marathon route prints** (Mark Your Moment, https://mrkyourmoment.com/en-us/collections/marathon-prints, opened 23 Sep 2026: "a bold city name at the top", route over a city grid, runner's name, time and distance at the bottom). `pattern inseparable from subject`: the value is a city's geography plus one person's 42 km. A four-metre problem has no map. Remove the map and all that's left is a name and a time.

## 3. Also worth making (ranked)

**1. "[Colour] to move."** This is pattern B, made for people nearby who have never tried. Four slides: the easiest circuit's start holds from standing height, then how to tell which holds belong to the problem, then the first move, then "this is what a V0 looks like from the ground". It deals with the fear that first-timer guides keep naming. SENDO (6 Jul 2026) says "nobody, genuinely nobody, is judging the person on the V0". This post takes the other half of that fear, not knowing what to do, and answers it by showing. *Fails if* it uses words like "crimp" without showing them.

**2. "Departures."** This is pattern D. A single static post, posted the same day each week. Ask the setting team for the dates. *Fails if* the ground colour changes from week to week, because the recognition is the point.

**3. "Front desk pick."** This is pattern C. One card, one named person, and one problem on the easiest circuits. The words have to be theirs. Change it every two weeks, as PW suggests. *Fails if* the pick is always the same person.

**4. For the gym's own reel: "What came down, what went up."** This comes from the pottery "weekly kiln diary: what went in, what came out, any surprises worth sharing" (https://www.socialmon.ai/blog/63-pottery-studio-post-ideas-with-examples-that-fill-classes-fast, opened 23 Sep 2026, undated). The gym films the stripped wall and then the new set from the same tripod position. This one works as a reel because the gym shoots its own video. It is not for your stills.
