## 1. Make this first: "Dry on the bench, wet in the glass"

**Why this one first:** the most common beginner mistake in the pages I read is using too much material. Dark Skies Fly Fishing lists "Use Less Material" first (4 April 2026), and The Essential Fly lists it too (23 October 2025). A sparse wet fly looks thin and unfinished on the bench, and that's why beginners over-dress it. This post shows them why they shouldn't. You need a fly you've already tied and a glass of water. On evidence against effort, this one wins.

**Format:** two-frame static, or a four-slide carousel. Pin the carousel cover to Pinterest.
**Cover:** a Partridge and Orange on the vise, dry. Shoot it square-on against a plain ground, with the fly taking up the middle third and nothing else in the frame.
**Slides:**
1. The claim: this fly looks under-dressed.
2. The same fly held under water in a clear glass, from the same angle and at the same size. It's the evidence the dry shot hides.
3. The two side by side, with one short label on each: *dry* and *wet*.
4. What to do at the vise: stop one turn of hackle sooner than feels right. Use your own number of turns.

**Hook:** a contradiction. The cover makes a claim that slide 2 overturns. Don't explain it on the cover.
**Caption job:** ask them to dunk the last fly they tied and say what changed.
**What would make it fail:**
- Changing the light, angle or scale between the two frames. The comparison only works if the water is the only thing that changes.
- Posting it before you've shot it. I haven't seen a wet partridge hackle photographed. One Flymph Forum member says Ephemera silk shows a "distinct darker color change" when wet (thread from December 2022). One aquarium test found hen, partridge and starling hackles all moved in water (Tenkara Talk, 2011). Both are other people's observations. If your wet frame doesn't look different, you don't have this post.

## 2. It works in another niche. Here is your version.

**① The crumb shot → the wet shot** *(this is the pattern behind §1)*
**Source:** sourdough bakers' "crumb shot". <creator> argues that "it is the texture of the crumb which ultimately dictates how good the bread is," and that books keep showing the crust instead. → dawesindoors.wordpress.com/2020/04/12/show-us-the-crumb-shot/, dated 12 April 2020. The Fresh Loaf post I opened (15 November 2017) runs exterior first, then crumb.
**The pattern:** frame 1 shows what the thing looks like. Frame 2 shows the state that proves whether it's any good, from the same viewpoint. The viewer learns to distrust frame 1.
**Your version:** frame 1 is the dry fly on the vise. Frame 2 is the fly in water, from the same angle and at the same size.
**Why it transfers:** a wet fly, like a loaf, is judged by a state the usual photo never shows.

**② Glaze test tiles → the hackle-turn strip**
**Source:** potters make tiles in a fixed grid and stamp a number into each one. Adelaide Barbey hangs "examples of each glaze on 4 different clay bodies" on her garage door. → suemcleodceramics.com/different-styles-of-test-tiles-you-can-make-for-glaze-testing/ (undated). A baker's version is the same loaf at 65, 70, 75 and 80% hydration. → flourwise.com/blog/sourdough-hydration-chart/, dated 28 January 2026.
**The pattern:** one thing is made several times with a single variable stepped. The results sit in a row, each with a small number under it, so the eye compares across instead of reading.
**Your version:** four Partridge and Orange flies, identical except for one to four turns of partridge. Lay them in a row on one ground, with a small numeral under each and nothing else.
**Slides:** cover, the row. 2, the two ends compared close up. 3, the same row wet (this links to ①). 4, which one you'd fish, and why.
**Hook:** open on the question the row answers, without answering it.
**Why it transfers:** "how much?" is a beginner's biggest question, and a test strip answers "how much" without words.

**③ Knolling → the recipe cover**
**Source:** <creator>'s rule "Always Be Knolling": remove what isn't in use, "group all like objects," and "align or square all objects to either the surface they rest on." → knolling.org/how-to-knoll/. Communication Arts adds that it's shot "straight down from above," with "equal spacing between them." → commarts.com/columns/the-flat-lay-on-knolling (undated).
**The pattern:** a list turned into a picture. Every part is squared to the table edge at equal gaps, shot from directly overhead, and nothing unused is in the frame.
**Your version:** the cover of every step-by-step. The hook goes in the centre, with the silk, the partridge feather, the wire and the dubbing squared around it at equal gaps, on your kitchen table, shot from directly above. Slide 2 onward is your normal step sequence. On Pinterest this cover is the pin.
**Leave alone:** the studio fetish, meaning tools and gear beyond what this one fly needs.
**Why it transfers:** a fly dressing already is a list. Knolling makes the list visible before the first turn of thread.

**④ The herbarium sheet → the specimen card**
**Source:** a botanist's label records the taxon, the collector ("leg."), the date and the locality. → herbariaunited.org/wiki/How_to_document_a_herbarium_sheet
**The pattern:** one specimen centred on a bare sheet, plus a small, dense label in a fixed corner. All the authority sits in the label. The specimen isn't styled.
**Your version:** the finished fly centred on plain card, with a small label in the lower right: the pattern's name, the hook, the silk, the hackle, the source (for example Pritt, *Yorkshire Trout Flies*, 1895, which is where Wikipedia says the Partridge and Orange was recorded), "tied by", and the date. The steps follow as slides.
**Why it transfers:** traditional wet flies come with a history. A specimen label turns that history into the layout, so it doesn't need a paragraph.

**⑤ The painted plate beside the tied fly** *(a note, not a refusal)*
**Source:** <creator> tied six salmon flies from the painted plates in Charles F. Orvis's *Fishing with the Fly* (1892), and showed each plate next to his fly. → partridge-of-redditch.co.uk/classic-fly-patterns-from-illusration/ (undated)
**Your version:** an old illustration of a North Country spider on the left and your tie on the right, at the same size.
**The ground problem:** you need a plate you're allowed to reproduce. Use a photo of a page from an old book you own, and check its copyright status yourself. I haven't.

**Refused: an aquarium comparison of hackles moving underwater.** → tenkaratalk.com/2011/09/video-underwater-comparison-of-sakasa-kebari-hackle/, dated 1 September 2011. The finding is that emu was "as stiff as a board" while partridge moved. Reason: `medium does not carry`. The whole point is the motion, and a still of a hackle mid-pulse is just a blurry photo. This belongs in your short reels, not in a carousel.

## 3. Also worth making

**1. The hackle-turn strip** (pattern ②). This is second because it takes an evening of tying. Four flies, one row, numerals only. Don't put a verdict on the cover.

**2. One proportion rule, one fly.** Sexyloops gives the soft-hackle rule as fibres "long enough to reach the hook point" (<creator>, sexyloops.com/flytying/proportion.shtml). Dark Skies (April 2026) has proportions as its fourth tip.
- **Cover:** the rule in words, big, over a single fly.
- **Slides:** 2, hackle too short. 3, hackle too long. 4, correct, with the hook point marked.
- **Hook:** the rule stated flatly. Tell them it's measured against the hook point, not guessed.
- **What would make it fail:** a different fly in each slide. Tie all three from the same batch.

**3. The knolled step-by-step for a winged wet** (pattern ③ as the cover). Winged wets are still being posted with their book source attached. On 3 May 2026 a Flymph Forum member posted a "Red Fly" from Willmarth's *Amateur Tyers Fly Dictionary*. The knolled cover suits a winged wet because it has more parts to lay out.
