# How to look

This is the part that is hard to copy. Anyone can return a list of links. What matters is whether
the thing that comes back can say **why** a piece of work is good, whether it has gone anywhere,
and what a different person could take from it.

---

## 1. Two axes, never added together

Read every piece of work on two separate axes. They are independent, they are **expected to
disagree**, and the disagreement is what you are looking for.

**Reach: how far it travelled**, judged against the author's own normal rather than as a raw
number. A post with 40,000 views from an account that always gets 40,000 travelled nowhere. The
question is always whether this one outran what they usually do.

In a browser you can rarely work that out properly, so say which basis you used:

| basis | how you got it | how much to trust it |
|---|---|---|
| **read** | the number is printed on the page you opened | good, with its date |
| **compared** | you opened five other posts by the same author and this one is visibly above them | fair; say how many you compared |
| **inferred** | you have a reason but no count: it is reposted widely, or cited in three roundups | weak, and it must be labelled |
| **unknown** | no basis at all | say unknown and leave it there |

**Unknown is an allowed and common answer.** Leave it as unknown. A guess does not close the gap.

**Craft: how well it is made.** "Is it pretty" is the wrong question. Craft is a breakdown, and
each part needs a sentence of evidence. On most hosts that evidence comes from one of two places:
the creator's own screenshots, or the page describing the work in words. Say which.

- **Composition**: the grid, what is symmetrical and what deliberately is not, how much of the
  frame is empty, where the eye lands first and second.
- **Colour**: how many colours are actually doing work, and whether there is one accent or four
  competing ones.
- **Typography**: the pairing, the size jump between levels, and how a second script is balanced
  against a Latin one when both appear.
- **Making**: how it was physically made. Paper cut, collage, 3D render, editorial photograph,
  hand lettering, scan, generated. Say which, and say whether you saw it or read it.
- **Originality against this niche's own norm**, rather than against everything. A restrained
  cream poster is unremarkable in ceramics and unusual on a gym's feed.
- **Coherence**: for a multi-slide set, whether slide two keeps the promise the cover made.

**Never produce one combined score.** A single number hides exactly the case you are looking for:
the well-made thing nobody has seen.

---

## 2. Register: a third read that orders, never scores

Craft asks whether a thing is well made. Register asks whether it belongs in *this* creator's
world. They are different questions, and a lot of bad recommendations answer only the first.

Read three dimensions on every reference, and on the creator's own work:

| | one end | the other |
|---|---|---|
| **ornament** | bare: one element, air around it | loaded: pattern, border, layered texture |
| **tone** | editorial: quiet, considered, adult | playful: bright, drawn, friendly |
| **carry** | type-led: the words hold the frame | photo-led: the picture holds it, words are caption |

**Derive the creator's register from their own work**, which on most hosts means their
screenshots. Do not derive it from what you assume the niche looks like. Two people posting about
the same subject can sit at opposite ends of all three.

**Register orders and never bans.** Work in the creator's register comes first. Work outside it
goes in a short separate run, *outside your register, worth seeing*. It stays, because the thing
that stretches someone is usually the thing they would not have picked. When you cannot read a
register, it is unknown: left unpenalised and unguessed.

There is one hard exclusion, and it is mechanical: something you could not open, a duplicate, or
a page that turned out to be something other than the work.

## 3. Well made, and what you can establish about it

The best version of this section is **high craft and low reach, both measured against this
niche's own norm**: the beautiful thing that has not travelled. That is the most useful thing a
director can hand over, and on a text-only host it is usually out of reach. You can't judge craft
by eye, and the reach numbers mostly sit behind logins. Say that plainly. Don't pretend.

So work with what you can actually establish, and label each reference with how you know:

- **seen**: you judged it from a screenshot the creator gave you. On a text-only host this is the
  only route to a real craft reading, which is why §1 of the skill asks for screenshots.
- **described**: the page says in words how it was made (a case study, a process post, a
  listing, alt text). Your director's note is built from that description and says so.
- **counted**: the page prints a public count (views, appreciations, plays). You read it and
  dated it.

A reference can qualify as *not yet travelled* only when its reach basis is **read** or
**compared** and the number is low for this niche. Everything else goes under **well made, reach
unknown**. That is an honest heading and a useful list.

Both halves are relative to the niche. In a type-led niche the well-made cover is usually the
restrained one: one line on a bare ground, one photograph used only as texture. In fashion the
same construction looks unfinished, and the well-made cover is a photograph that took a studio.
Judge the first by the second's standard and the type-led niche loses every time just for being
what it is. Reach works the same way: a save count that would be unremarkable on a recipe wall can
be the top of the distribution in a niche of two hundred accounts.

Rules that keep it honest:

- **One entry per creator.** Otherwise a single prolific designer fills the section and it becomes
  a fan page.
- **Recent work wins ties.** Something well made and four years old has had its chance.
- **If you have nothing seen, described or counted, the section is two lines**: what you looked
  for, and what stopped you. Do not fill it with adjectives about pictures you never saw.
- **The craft bar comes first, and it is a real bar.** Low view counts alone earn nothing here.

To check your reading, show the creator what you called well made. If it is not what they would
have saved, your reading is wrong for this person, and you adjust to them.

## 4. Adaptation: "it works in another niche, here is your version"

A creator's most common real question is *that worked over there, so what is my version of it?*
It comes up far more often than *what is trending*, and it is the brief's lead section. An answer
has five parts, and skipping any one turns it back into a caption.

**1. Name the pattern apart from its subject.** This is the hard part and most of the value.

> ✗ "a recipe card"
> ✓ "a numbered rule laid over a single photograph, with the number set several times larger
> than the words, and the photograph desaturated so the number is the only contrast in the frame"

The first is the source's topic. The second is a construction, and a construction can travel.

**2. Put it in the creator's own vocabulary**, using the words you collected in Pass 1 rather
than your general knowledge of the subject.

> "For the coffee shop: one brewing number per card, the resting days for this week's bag set at
> three times the size of the line that explains them, over a single photograph of the bar with
> the contrast dropped so the number is the only bright thing in the frame."

**3. Plan the slides by role**, if it is a multi-slide set. "Slide 2: more info" is a
placeholder. Give each slide its job. Cover: the claim. Slide 2: the exception that makes the
rule interesting. Slide 3: the worked example. Last slide: what to do with it. One line per role.

**4. Rewrite the hook** in the creator's register. Take the *shape* of the original line and leave
its words: if it opens with a number, open with a number; if it opens with a contradiction, find
the contradiction in this subject. Never put an invented figure in the creator's mouth ("it took
me 31 tries"). If the hook needs a number, it has to be the creator's own, and the brief says so.

**5. Say why it transfers**, in one sentence, naming the property that carries.

Put **the real reference beside every adaptation**: the page you opened, and its date. An
adaptation without its source is a claim nobody can check.

### Refuse rather than invent, and say why

An adaptation that cannot be made is worse than none, because the reader will try it. Three named
refusals:

- **`medium does not carry`**: the source is a video whose value is motion or timing, and the
  reader makes still images. A carousel cannot inherit a cut.
- **`pattern inseparable from subject`**: take the subject away and nothing is left. A
  before-and-after of a renovated kitchen shows a kitchen, and there is no pattern left to carry.
- **`the reader cannot produce the ground`**: the construction needs an original photograph, a
  studio, or a face the reader does not have.

Treat the third as a note before it becomes a refusal. "You would need a photograph or a stand-in:
a scan of the bag, a piece of textured paper, a photograph of the bar" is useful. Dropping the
reference without a word helps nobody, and neither does pretending the problem isn't there.

## 5. The director's note

Every reference gets one line, and that line shows more clearly than anything else whether the
work was done properly.

> ✗ "Mostly near-black with pale grey, high contrast, low text density."
> ✓ "One line of type at sixty per cent of the frame width; the photograph is only ground, and
> the drop in contrast is what lets the type sit on it without an outline."

The first describes pixels. The second tells a person how the thing was built and what to do with
it. **A palette is never the reason.** Write what a director would say standing at a wall: what is
doing the work, what is deliberately absent, and what you would take.

Also say what to leave alone. Most references have a part that only works because of who made it
or what it is about. Naming that part turns a reference into an instruction.

If the note is built from the page's own description rather than from a screenshot, write
"(from the maker's description)" after it.

---

## 6. Order of work, and where the time goes

Roughly:

| | |
|---|---|
| the creator's own references and screenshots, and their register | 10% |
| the niche's own vocabulary and what is moving | 25% |
| the design pass: artefact vocabulary × subject, reading what pages say about how things were made | 20% |
| other niches and the transfer tests | 30% |
| writing the brief | 15% |

If most of your time goes on what is trending, you have written a keyword report, and the creator
already has one of those. The other niches and the transfer tests are where the brief earns its
place, so they get the biggest share.
